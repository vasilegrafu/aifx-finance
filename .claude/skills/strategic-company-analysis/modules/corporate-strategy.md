# corporate-strategy — whether the parent adds value to the portfolio

## Question this answers

Why should these businesses belong under the same corporate parent — and does
the parent make them worth more than they would be apart?

## When to run / when to skip

Run only for multi-business companies, or when diversification, spin-offs, or
portfolio questions are asked. Skip for single-business companies — their
capital-allocation questions belong to
`management-and-capital-allocation.md`.

## Inputs

- Business-level analyses (at minimum `business-model.md` per major unit, or
  honest one-paragraph sketches where depth is not warranted).
- Segment economics from the fact pack where reported.

## Method

1. **State the portfolio logic as a claim.** Related diversification (shared
   capabilities, customers, or costs), vertical integration, geographic
   spread, or financial conglomerate. Whatever it is, phrase it as a testable
   claim: "unit B is worth more here because the parent contributes X."
2. **Test parenting value concretely.** Where does the parent demonstrably
   add value — shared capabilities that transfer, cross-selling that shows up
   in revenue, procurement or infrastructure scale, a brand that carries,
   cheaper internal capital, genuinely better capital allocation? Where does
   it subtract — complexity cost, blended incentives, cross-subsidies that
   hide failing units, capital trapped in the wrong business?
3. **Better-off test, both directions.** Is each business better off owned
   by this parent than by its best alternative owner — and is the parent
   better off owning it than selling it at today's price?
4. **Name the simplification candidates.** Units that fail the test are
   divestment, spin-off, or run-for-cash candidates; say which, and what the
   obstacle is (entanglement, shared systems, guarantees, sentiment).

## What good looks like

> The stated logic is related diversification; the observed logic is a
> financial one — the cash cow funds two subscale ventures whose only shared
> asset with it is the logo. The parenting test fails in both directions: the
> ventures would raise better-priced capital from owners who understand them,
> and the cow's cash would return more in buybacks than in subsidies
> [Inference; segment margins and capex from filings]. This is a portfolio
> held together by history. The strategic question is not how to run it
> better but whether to keep it assembled.

## Hands off

- The parenting verdict and any failed better-off tests →
  `synthesis/diagnosis.md` (often a top-three issue by magnitude).
- Divestment / spin-off candidates → `synthesis/alternatives.md` as concrete
  options with rough proceeds arithmetic.
- Cross-subsidies that distort unit economics → `financial-performance.md`.

## Failure modes

- "Synergy" asserted without a line item — a transfer that cannot be located
  in revenue or cost does not exist.
- Testing only one direction of better-off — a business can be fine and still
  be worth more to someone else.
- Treating the portfolio as given and only optimizing within it.
- Diversification judged by relatedness of products instead of relatedness of
  capabilities and customers.
