# growth-and-ma — which growth creates value, and whether to build, buy, or partner

## Question this answers

Which of the realistic growth options would create value above the cost of
capital — and for each, should the company build, buy, or partner?

## When to run / when to skip

Run when growth, expansion, diversification, or a specific deal is on the
table, or when a full analysis has established that the core is maturing.
Skip when the diagnosis points the other way — a company below its cost of
capital in the core has no business analyzing adjacencies until that is
explained.

## Inputs

- **Which capabilities transfer** — from `competitive-advantage.md`. This is
  the whole game: an adjacency is attractive only where an existing advantage
  travels to it.
- Industry attractiveness of candidate markets — `industry-structure.md`
  logic applied briefly to each.
- Balance-sheet capacity and current ROIC from `financial-performance.md` /
  the fact pack.

## Method

1. **Enumerate the realistic options** across the standard axes —
   penetration, new segments, new products, new geographies, adjacent
   markets, platform extension, vertical integration — but keep only ones
   with a stated reason this company specifically would win there. "The
   market is large" is not a reason.
2. **Score each option on the questions that kill deals**: structural
   attractiveness of the target market; which existing advantage transfers
   (and which stays home — distribution rarely travels, brand sometimes,
   process capabilities most often); capital required and time to impact;
   execution difficulty against known capability gaps; cannibalization of the
   core; and the competitive response the move invites.
3. **Apply rule 3 explicitly.** Sketch the arithmetic: rough capital in,
   plausible returns, versus cost of capital. Growth that fails this sketch
   is value-destroying however strategic it sounds. Include "return the cash"
   as the baseline every option must beat.
4. **Build vs buy vs partner**, per surviving option: build when the
   capability exists and time permits; buy when speed or a scarce asset
   demands it — priced against the well-documented tendency of acquirers to
   pay away the synergies; partner when the capability is needed but control
   is not, accepting the dependency created.
5. **For a specific deal**: strategic rationale in one sentence, synergies
   with arithmetic (which line item, how much, by when), integration
   complexity against the company's *demonstrated* integration capability
   (from `management-and-capital-allocation.md`), and the
   what-must-be-true price test.

## What good looks like

> Geographic expansion survives only in the two markets where the regulatory
> certification already held is the entry barrier — there the advantage
> travels intact. The larger, more discussed market fails the sketch: entry
> demands local manufacturing (~$B capital) into a structure with stronger
> buyer power, for returns that under generous assumptions roughly meet the
> cost of capital — a lot of execution risk to earn the baseline
> [Assumption: capital estimate from comparable entries]. Buy-vs-build: build,
> because the certification is the asset and it is already owned; the
> acquisition targets on offer sell distribution, which is the one thing that
> doesn't need buying.

## Hands off

- Surviving options with their sketch arithmetic →
  `synthesis/alternatives.md` as candidate strategies.
- Capital requirements → `financial-performance.md` capacity check.
- The competitive responses each move invites →
  `synthesis/alternatives.md`'s post-retaliation test.

## Failure modes

- Growth assumed desirable — unquantified expansion into an unexamined
  structure.
- Adjacency chosen by market size instead of advantage transfer.
- The sketch skipped: no option evaluated against cost of capital or against
  simply returning the cash.
- M&A rationale that is really empire arithmetic; synergies asserted without
  a line item; integration capability assumed rather than demonstrated.
- Cannibalization and competitor response left out of an otherwise positive
  case.
