# industry-structure — whether the game itself is worth winning

## Question this answers

Is the industry structurally attractive, which forces set its profitability,
and is this company's profit explained by the industry or by its position in
it?

## When to run / when to skip

Run in standard and full analyses, and standalone for "is this a good
industry", entry decisions, or "why are margins like this" questions. Skip
when the question is entirely company-internal and the industry verdict is
already established in the conversation.

## Inputs

- The **profit-engine segment** from `business-model.md` — run the forces at
  that level; industry structure for a blend of businesses is a category
  error.
- External forces that alter the structure, from `external-environment.md`.
- Standalone: define the market first (product scope, geography, tier) — a
  five-forces answer changes with the market definition, so state it.

## Method

Porter's five forces, each held to the same standard — mechanism, not label:

1. **Rivalry** — concentration, differentiation vs commoditization, fixed-cost
   pressure to fill capacity, exit barriers, growth (rivalry hardens when
   growth stops). Who actually cuts price first, and why?
2. **New entrants** — what specifically keeps them out: scale thresholds,
   switching costs, distribution access, regulation, capital, brand. Barriers
   are measured by the economics a real entrant faces, not by incumbency age.
3. **Supplier power** — concentration relative to the industry, switching
   costs, credibility of forward integration, share of the cost structure.
4. **Buyer power** — concentration, price sensitivity, switching costs,
   credibility of backward integration or self-supply, how informed buyers
   are.
5. **Substitutes** — the different way of getting the same job done, its
   price-performance trajectory, and the customer's cost of moving to it.

For every force: the mechanism → its effect on industry profitability → its
direction (strengthening/weakening, and why) → who is advantaged → how this
company responds.

**Verdict, in two parts:** (a) structurally attractive or not, and where
profit pools sit; (b) the attribution — how much of this company's
profitability the *industry* explains versus its *position*. Part (b) is the
question the advantage module inherits.

## What good looks like

> Rivalry is the binding force, and it is hardening for a structural reason:
> the industry added capacity against demand that has stopped growing, the
> plants are high-fixed-cost, and exit is blocked by remediation liabilities —
> so every downturn now converts into a price war rather than capacity
> leaving. Attribution: the industry explains little of the company's margin;
> its spread over peers comes from position (captive distribution), which is
> exactly what the advantage module must now test for durability.

## Hands off

- The **attribution verdict** (industry vs position) →
  `competitive-advantage.md` — it defines how much advantage there is to
  explain.
- Structural attractiveness and profit-pool location →
  `synthesis/diagnosis.md` and `growth-and-ma.md` (which adjacencies have
  better structure).
- Forces that are strengthening → `scenarios-and-risk.md` as downside
  mechanisms.

## Failure modes

- High/medium/low labels with no economics — the ban the original skill got
  right; keep it absolute.
- Running the forces on a blended multi-segment company.
- An undefined market, so every force answer is unfalsifiable.
- Static analysis: a force's direction matters more than its level.
- Skipping attribution — declaring the industry attractive says nothing yet
  about whether *this company's* returns are positional or inherited.
