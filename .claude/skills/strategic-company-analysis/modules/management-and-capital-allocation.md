# management-and-capital-allocation — are the allocators good, and are they aligned

## Question this answers

Is the team allocating this company's capital demonstrably good at it, and do
their incentives point at value creation or at something else?

## When to run / when to skip

Run in every full analysis and standalone for management-quality, governance,
or buyback/dividend questions. The strategy and the strategists are inseparable
— a good strategy under misaligned allocators rarely survives contact with a
budget. Skip only for pure industry questions with no company attached.

## Inputs

- Capital-allocation history from the fact pack (capex, buybacks, dividends,
  acquisitions over the window) — read, not recalled.
- Proxy statements / remuneration reports for incentive structure; past
  guidance vs delivered results from filings and transcripts.
- Deal history from `growth-and-ma.md` if already run.

## Method

1. **Judge the track record, not the narrative.** Where did the last five to
   ten years of capital actually go — and what did each destination return?
   Acquisitions: subsequent impairments, retained or divested, revenue kept?
   Buybacks: concentrated at low or high valuations? Capex: did the invested
   capital earn its cost (tie to `financial-performance.md`)? Dividends:
   sustainable from free cash flow or borrowed?
2. **Read the incentives as revealed preferences.** What does compensation
   actually pay for — EPS (buyback-gameable), revenue growth (size, not
   value), TSR vs peers, ROIC? Time horizons of vesting vs the strategy's
   time-to-impact. Insider ownership: skin in the game or options upside
   only? The agency lens in one question: where do this team's interests
   *diverge* from owners', and is observed behavior consistent with the
   divergence?
3. **Test credibility.** Past guidance against delivered results; whether
   language changed when facts changed; whether failed initiatives were named
   as failures or quietly renamed. Candor is measurable across a few years of
   letters and calls.
4. **Governance red flags, briefly**: board independence and tenure, related
   -party transactions, dual-class structures and who they entrench, auditor
   or CFO churn. Flag; do not pad.
5. **Verdict**: allocation capability (strong / adequate / value-destroying),
   alignment (aligned / mixed / misaligned), with the one or two behaviors
   that decide it — and what this team can *credibly execute*, which bounds
   the strategies worth recommending.

## What good looks like

> The narrative is transformation; the ledger says otherwise. Of ~$9B
> deployed over five years [Fact: fact pack, cumulative], two-thirds went to
> buybacks executed fairly evenly — including through the period the stock
> traded at twice today's multiple — and the three transformation
> acquisitions were written down within three years. Compensation pays on
> adjusted EPS, which the buybacks mechanically raise. Nothing here is
> improper; it is simply a team paid to shrink the share count, behaving as
> paid. Verdict: allocation adequate-mechanical, alignment mixed — a strategy
> requiring patient multi-year investment would fight the incentive plan
> itself, and should not be recommended without flagging that
> [Inference].

## Hands off

- **Execution credibility** → `synthesis/recommendation.md` (feasibility of
  anything multi-year) and `synthesis/alternatives.md` (which options this
  team can actually run).
- Incentive misalignments → `scenarios-and-risk.md` as organizational risk.
- Allocation record → `financial-performance.md` (does deployed capital earn
  its cost).

## Failure modes

- Judging management by articulateness on earnings calls.
- The halo effect: extrapolating one celebrated success across all
  allocation.
- Ignoring incentives — behavior follows the comp plan more reliably than the
  strategy deck.
- Governance items listed exhaustively instead of the two that bind.
- Recommending a strategy this team's incentives will quietly strangle,
  without saying so.
