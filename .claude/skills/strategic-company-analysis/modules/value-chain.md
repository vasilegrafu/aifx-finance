# value-chain — which activities the economics actually live in

## Question this answers

In which activities do this company's cost or differentiation advantages
actually arise, and which activities must stay internal versus be bought?

## When to run / when to skip

Run when the source of a cost or differentiation edge is still unclear after
the advantage module, or when the question involves vertical integration,
outsourcing, or a make-vs-buy choice. Skip otherwise — this is the most
selective module; a full activity map of a company whose advantage is already
located adds pages, not insight.

## Inputs

- The advantage candidates from `competitive-advantage.md` — this module
  locates them in specific activities.
- Cost structure signals from the fact pack (gross vs operating margin, capex
  and R&D intensity) when figures are cited.

## Method

1. **Map only the strategic activities.** Inputs and procurement, operations,
   logistics, marketing and sales, distribution, service, technology, people
   systems — but only the ones where this company plausibly differs from
   rivals. Skip the rest by name ("logistics: parity, not analyzed").
2. **Locate the advantage.** For each differing activity: does it create a
   cost gap or a willingness-to-pay gap, roughly how large, and is the gap
   caused by the activity itself or by how activities *link* (linkages are
   harder to imitate than any single activity)?
3. **Find the bottlenecks and dependencies.** The activity that caps
   throughput or quality; the input, supplier, or platform whose loss would
   halt the chain; concentration risks worth naming.
4. **Make the boundary argument.** For integration or outsourcing questions,
   reason it as economics, not fashion: transaction costs and hold-up risk
   (asset-specific investments favor integration), capability protection (an
   activity feeding a differentiating capability is dangerous to hand to a
   supplier who serves rivals), and capital returns (integration adds invested
   capital — rule 3 applies to the integrated activity too).

## What good looks like

> The cost advantage does not sit in manufacturing, where the company pays the
> same contract rates as everyone — it sits in the linkage between design and
> procurement: components are designed around parts already at scale for
> other product lines, so purchasing leverage compounds across the range. That
> linkage is organizational, which is why rivals who copied any single design
> have not closed the ~4pt gross-margin gap [Inference; margins read from
> fact pack]. It also settles the outsourcing question: design must stay
> internal; assembly is already effectively external.

## Hands off

- The located, activity-level source of advantage → back to
  `competitive-advantage.md`'s causal chain (the "what creates it" link).
- Integration and outsourcing options with their economics →
  `growth-and-ma.md` and `corporate-strategy.md`.
- Bottlenecks and single-point dependencies → `scenarios-and-risk.md`.

## Failure modes

- The full textbook chain drawn for its own sake — nine boxes, no location of
  advantage.
- Treating an advantage as living in one activity when it lives in a linkage.
- Make-vs-buy argued from fashion ("asset-light") instead of hold-up risk,
  capability protection, and return on the capital involved.
- Ignoring that integration must itself clear the cost of capital.
