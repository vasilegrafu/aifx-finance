# output-forms — how the finished analysis is written

Load before writing any deliverable. The governing rule: **the document is
structured by the argument, not by the module list.** Modules are how the
analysis was *done*; the report is how the diagnosis is *told*. A reader
should never be able to reconstruct the pipeline from the section headings.

## Full report (full tier)

Unless the user requests another format:

> **Strategic Analysis of [Company]** — dated, with the as-of date of the data
>
> **Executive summary** — see standard below
> 1. **Company and business model** — how it creates and captures value
> 2. **Strategic context** — the external changes that matter, and why
> 3. **Industry structure** — the forces that set profitability, and the
>    industry-vs-position attribution
> 4. **Competitive position and advantage** — why customers choose it, where
>    the advantage comes from, whether it is durable *(one argument — the
>    two modules merge here deliberately)*
> 5. **Capabilities and organization** — what enables or constrains strategy,
>    including management and capital allocation
> 6. **Financial performance** — outcomes tied to strategic causes
> 7. **Corporate and growth strategy** — portfolio, expansion, M&A, where
>    relevant
> 8. **Key strategic issues** — the diagnosis: 3–5 issues, sized and ranked
> 9. **Strategic alternatives** — genuinely different options, with the
>    post-retaliation economics
> 10. **Recommended strategy** — the choice, its defense, what must be true
> 11. **Implementation** — 0–12 / 12–24 / 24–60 months, with KPIs
> 12. **Risks, scenarios, and falsification** — the scenario set and the
>     falsification table, together
>
> **Final strategic judgment** — one paragraph: what must this company do to
> create and sustain superior economic value over the next 3–5 years?

## Executive summary standard

Not a compressed table of contents — a self-sufficient argument answering, in
order: **where are we / why are we here / where should we go / why / what must
we do / what could make us wrong**. An executive who reads nothing else must
still receive the diagnosis, the recommendation, and the biggest caveat. If
the summary could be written without the analysis, it is not a summary of it.

## Quick take (one page)

Four blocks, no headings needed: **position** (business model + why customers
choose it, one paragraph) · **advantage verdict** (durable / contested /
temporary, with the mechanism) · **central tension** (the one decision that
matters) · **what to watch** (2–3 observable indicators with thresholds).
Label evidence inline; if the fact pack ran, cite the as-of date once.

## Standard memo (2–4 pages)

Executive summary → diagnosis (the 3–5 sized issues) → recommendation with
what-must-be-true → falsification table. The analytical modules appear only
as evidence citations inside those sections, never as sections of their own.

## Comparative form (two or more companies)

Compare on the decisions, not the descriptions: same peer set, same anchored
scoring frame, and a verdict per dimension that names the *mechanism* of the
difference. One table of anchored scores, then prose only where the companies
genuinely differ. Never two full reports stapled together.

## Academic mode

Add on top of any form: define the strategic concepts used; connect the
frameworks explicitly rather than deploying them separately; distinguish
theory from company evidence; make assumptions a named section; cite sources
per the hub's tier discipline. Theoretical lenses (Porter, RBV/VRIO, dynamic
capabilities, transaction costs, agency, disruption, network effects,
parenting) appear only where they carry explanatory weight — a lens that
merely renames a finding is cut.

## Tables, and when they earn a place

A table earns its place when it compresses a *comparison* (competitors,
options, scenarios, the falsification table, anchored scores). It does not
earn a place by a framework existing. Prose owns causality; tables own
comparison; never let a table stand in for the explanation of a mechanism.

## Writing standard

Direct, analytical, decisive without overclaiming. "The advantage is
weakening because…" — never "there are several factors that may potentially
impact…". Calibration vocabulary, used precisely: *the evidence suggests / the
strongest explanation is / a plausible alternative is / the critical
uncertainty is / this depends on / there is insufficient evidence to
conclude*. Every load-bearing claim carries its label (Fact / Inference /
Assumption / Hypothesis) or an inline source. Short sentences carry
conclusions; subordinate clauses carry caveats, not the other way around.
