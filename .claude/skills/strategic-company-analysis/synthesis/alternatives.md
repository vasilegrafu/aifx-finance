# alternatives — genuinely different ways to resolve the diagnosis

## Question this answers

What are three to five genuinely different strategies that could resolve the
diagnosed issues, and how does each survive contact with competitors, capital,
and this management team?

## When to run / when to skip

Run after `synthesis/diagnosis.md` in a full analysis, or standalone when the
user brings a decision ("should we enter X / buy Y / defend Z") — in which
case reconstruct a one-paragraph diagnosis first, because options are answers
to issues. Skip in quick takes.

## Inputs

- Prioritized issues and the central tension from `synthesis/diagnosis.md`.
- Capability gaps (`competitive-advantage.md`), financial capacity
  (`financial-performance.md`), execution credibility
  (`management-and-capital-allocation.md`), option sketches
  (`growth-and-ma.md`), robust-vs-bet classification (`scenarios-and-risk.md`).

## Method

1. **Generate genuinely different options** — different answers to where to
   compete or how to win, not one strategy at three intensities. A useful
   spread: a **defend/deepen** option, a **transform/reposition** option, a
   **shrink-to-the-core/harvest** option, plus what the diagnosis specifically
   invites. Include the honest baseline: change nothing, return surplus cash —
   every option must beat it, and naming it keeps the others honest.
2. **Specify each option identically**: objective; the three or four core
   actions; required capabilities against the known gaps; capital required
   vs available; expected value with rough arithmetic (rule 6); main risks;
   organizational implications; time to impact; the key assumptions it rests
   on. Symmetric depth — an option sketched thinly is a decision made
   covertly.
3. **Run the post-retaliation test on every serious option.** Who loses if
   this works → what retaliation is available to them (price, bundling,
   poaching, litigation, lobbying, copying) → how fast → and what the
   option's economics look like **after** the response, not before. An option
   priced pre-retaliation is mispriced. Asymmetric rivals are the good news
   case: the best moves are ones competitors *cannot* follow without
   damaging their own model — say when that is true.
4. **Evaluate on a common frame** — attractiveness, strategic fit,
   feasibility (capabilities × capital × this management), risk, time to
   impact, durability of the resulting position. A scoring table is welcome
   *if* every score carries its reason in a phrase; naked numbers are
   decoration. No false precision: 1–5 with reasons beats 1–100 without.

## What good looks like

> Option B (move down-market to stop the insurgent) fails the retaliation
> test, not the spreadsheet: the insurgent's model is profitable at price
> points that require our fixed-cost base to be unprofitable, so it can
> follow any cut we make and we cannot follow its last one. Post-retaliation,
> B converts a niche loss into a margin war we finance for both sides. Option
> C (cede the low end, deepen the certified segment where switching costs
> bind) survives: retaliation there requires certifications that take a rival
> ~3 years to earn [Fact: regulatory timeline], and the incumbent advantage
> compounds meanwhile. C beats the do-nothing baseline by roughly [arithmetic
> shown]; B does not.

## Hands off

- The ranked options with post-retaliation economics →
  `synthesis/recommendation.md`.
- Each option's key assumptions → `synthesis/falsification.md`.
- The rejected options and *why* → `synthesis/recommendation.md`, which must
  say why the alternatives are inferior.

## Failure modes

- Three intensities of one idea wearing different names.
- The do-nothing baseline omitted — every option then looks necessary.
- Options priced before retaliation.
- Feasibility checked against an idealized organization instead of the
  capability gaps and incentive plan already on file.
- Scores without reasons; precision without basis.
- The favored option specified in detail and the rest as strawmen.
